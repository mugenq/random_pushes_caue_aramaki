# Human Interaction Should Be Worth More Than a Bottle of Water

> **LLM text | Human AI** · Original concepts and mechanism by Mugenq / Caue Aramaki; prose organized and expanded with an LLM  
> **Provenance:** Original seed proposition plus the author's October 2026 clarification of its mechanism and governance problem. Explicit authorial positions are distinguished below from editorial analysis.  
> **Status:** Speculative post-labor social-system proposal, not an implemented program, economic forecast, or fully resolved political theory.

## 1. From commodity value to human and cultural value

The starting intuition is that **human interaction should eventually be worth more than a bottle of water**.

The point is not a literal exchange-rate claim about conversations and bottled water. In an imagined future where food, water, housing, basic amenities, and the output of heavy industry become abundant and inexpensive, material sustenance would no longer need to dominate people's lives. The remaining scarce or significant contributions could increasingly concern human culture: keeping practices alive, finding new interests, teaching, participating, creating, and maintaining a shared **cultural library**.

In this vision, the relative importance of everyday human participation rises as the cost of necessities falls.

The specific proposal that follows was supplied by the author *after* the original brief sketch.

## 2. Equal sustenance input, unequal human output

Under today's meritocratic or capitalist arrangements, income is often tied to productive output. The author points out a tension: **human beings require broadly similar basic sustenance, but produce vastly different economic outputs**.

In a possible future where automation trivializes many jobs, requiring everyone to justify their food and shelter by measured economic productivity becomes increasingly detached from the material reality of abundance. Beyond a long tail of difficult work, much of the activity still worth sustaining may be cultural participation and preservation.

The proposal therefore **flattens the reward for ordinary participation** rather than calibrating each person's basic allotment to their productivity, prestige, or influence.

## 3. A binary role-participation rule

The mechanism is deliberately simple:

- **Are you active in a recognized role?** If yes, receive a basic package: meals, necessary expenses, amenities, and access to somewhere to live.
- **Not participating in a role?** Under the proposed rule, receive no individual allocation through this system.
- **What counts as a role?** Not only technically or economically valuable work. Practicing **kendo**, participating in or sustaining a cultural community, and similar pursuits would count.
- **High-value or arduous jobs:** People who perform unusually difficult work for the system receive the **same baseline sustenance**, with perhaps a limited, harmless additional incentive.

The author compared participation certification to **signing for a scholarship each month**: an ongoing signal of activity, not a competition to prove whose role deserves the most food.

This is a **conditional distribution rule**, not unconditional basic income. The author acknowledged that somebody who does not participate might rely on family, while imagining a society in which basic sustenance is so inexpensive that supplying another person would not be a serious burden.

That last assumption is essential to the proposal and has not been demonstrated. People without families or supportive communities would pose a serious unresolved problem.

## 4. Make gaming the system harmless

The author explicitly **accepts people pretending to participate**.

Someone could join a fashionable cultural scene without sincerely loving it, choose an easy role, or *LARP* an identity. That is not, by itself, a design failure. While pretending, the person might discover a community or practice that genuinely resonates with them. If they never do, they can still move on; cultural interests and trends will shift unpredictably anyway.

The aim is to make the hardship of participation so low, and its basic reward so flat, that **searching for the cheapest exploit offers almost no advantage over simply pursuing an interest**.

The intended paths of least resistance are:

1. **Do what I want.**
2. **Fake it until I make it.**

This is a different response to reward hacking from elaborate surveillance or increasingly strict verification. Instead of trying to detect every insincere participant, the system tries to make insincerity cheap, tolerable, and sometimes accidentally productive.

**Editorial interpretation, not a claimed theorem:** A useful general design principle here is to *reduce the payoff gap between gaming a metric and fulfilling its intended purpose*. The proposal does not prove that every exploit becomes harmless; it deliberately tolerates certain ones.

## 5. Cultural trends and the shared library

Cultural contributions need not mean publishing masterworks or receiving approval from an elite institution. The author imagines people **flocking to fashionable cultures**, practicing, belonging, imitating, changing their minds, and sometimes discovering what they actually care about.

Even transient involvement could help transmit practices, maintain communities, and expose people to unfamiliar forms of life. Because the baseline sustenance is not proportional to supposed cultural greatness, the system need not judge the objective quality of every contribution just to feed someone.

The **cultural library** is the collective object of preservation: living activities and knowledge, not merely a physical collection of archived files.

Exactly which actions constitute a qualifying role, how cultural preservation is identified, and whether a given community is being helped remain open.

## 6. The governance problem: who defines a role?

The author identified **governance as the central unsolved problem**, describing the necessary authority as almost **deity-like**.

Someone or something must decide which roles qualify, administer certification, distribute resources, settle disputes, and prevent the institution itself from becoming coercive. Even where resources are abundant and individual rewards are equal, control over the *definition of participation* is enormous power.

One possible large-scale administrator would be an **overseer AI**. The author regards that as a plausible option and simultaneously a **deeply troubling one**, anticipating that many people would reject such an authority. This is **not** an endorsement of AI rule.

An AI might scale administrative classification, but scale does not confer political legitimacy, consent, accountability, or trustworthy value judgments.

**Editorial alternatives to investigate, not solutions endorsed by the author:** decentralized communities, democratic rule-setting, appeal rights, constitutional limits, and dividing certification from allocation. Each option leaves difficult questions of authority and coordination.

## 7. Unresolved objections and implementation questions

These concerns are included to expose the model's unfinished parts, **not to attribute their answers to the author**.

- **The zero-allocation cliff:** What happens to someone between roles, isolated, severely ill, disabled, disoriented, or unable to participate? A family's charity is not a dependable safety net.
- **Abundance as a precondition:** How inexpensive must food, housing, water, energy, and public infrastructure become before the mechanism is viable? How would scarcity or supply shocks affect it?
- **Governance capture:** Who recognizes a legitimate role? Could administrators exclude unpopular cultures, dissenters, or minority practices?
- **Certification and privacy:** How can participation be registered without building surveillance or a punitive bureaucracy?
- **Migration and membership:** Who belongs to the allocating community, and on what basis?
- **Role quality versus role presence:** If fake participation is accepted, what exactly must the binary test check?
- **Intrinsic human worth:** Does making an allocation conditional risk confusing public support with a person's moral worth or right to live?

The final question is an especially important distinction: **this document describes a speculative distribution mechanism, not a claim that nonparticipants deserve deprivation or have less human dignity**. Whether the binary rule can protect vulnerable people is unresolved.

## 8. Current formulation

The revised proposal can be summarized as follows:

> In a highly automated society where basic sustenance is cheap, allocate a roughly equal package of necessities to anyone participating in a recognized social or cultural role. Accept easy roles, imitation, and even insincere participation, because exploring cultures can itself be beneficial and because reward-gaming should be made nearly pointless. The most difficult unsolved problem is the legitimacy and power of whichever institution, human or AI, decides what counts as participation.

**Author's unresolved endpoint:** The original exchange briefly cut off while describing why ongoing cultural participation matters. Later messages clarify the incentive mechanism and governance concern, but do not amount to a complete theory or policy specification.
