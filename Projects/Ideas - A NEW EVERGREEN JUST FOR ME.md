# A NEW EVERGREEN JUST FOR ME

## Premise

An adult womanizer and opportunist, provisionally called **Machiavelli**, crosses into an isekai setting after intruding on a sacred funeral ritual. His life takes him into the orbit of nobility, faith, and the paladins. Unlike blessed heroes, he has **no magic**: competence, endurance, and defensive strength must be earned through effort.

## The character who changes

The desired arc is not sudden moral purity. The former playboy retains cunning, social perceptiveness, humor, fatigue and recognizable vices. He grows into a flawed but compassionate older mentor, with some of the worldly restraint of Sein from *Frieren* and the protective mentorship of Jiraiya.

A disciple helps heal the mentor's emotional life. The disciple teaches him to love people and take pleasure in their continued existence; the mentor's final act, in turn, preserves other lives.

## The sacrifice

A corrupt religious power unleashes a **country-scale blast of holy light**. The magicless defender acts as a shield for civilians and the people near him. The moment is not victory through a newly discovered blessing. It is the culmination of chosen effort and care.

A later glimpse, **one hundred years afterward**, reveals an evergreen tree. An earlier criticism of trees as parasitic gains an inverted meaning: his final legacy shelters rather than takes.

## Motifs and remembered fragments

- Conditional mercy and atonement rather than “chosen one” destiny.
- **“Don't give up.”**
- **“Stay radiant as ever.”**
- **“I think my only regret would be trying to live thinking I wouldn't regret.”**
- A dog-related moment that challenges the judgment implied by a warning sign.
- An earlier image of trees taking nutrients and blocking sunlight, reversed in the ending.

These remembered lines and motifs are **notes requiring verification against the author's preferred script**, not a locked shooting draft.

## The moral geometry

The unblessed person who was initially selfish becomes an enduring source of shelter. The story asks whether goodness is received as destiny or deliberately chosen through effort, attachment, and regret.
